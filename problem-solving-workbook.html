Why Do Everyday Problems Feel So Difficult to Solve?

Most of us were never taught how to work through a problem. We just react, and hope it works out. You may recognise some of these:

  • Feeling stuck when facing a difficult problem.
  • Overthinking decisions without making progress.
  • Struggling to identify the real cause of a problem.
  • Repeating the same mistakes.
  • Having too many ideas but not knowing which one to pursue.
  • Making decisions without fully understanding the situation.
  • Knowing something needs to change but not knowing where to begin.

None of this means something is wrong with you. It usually means the problem hasn't been looked at in a structured way yet.

A Better Way to Approach Problems

Problem-Solving Questions, Answered

What is problem-solving?

Problem-solving is the process of understanding a situation that isn't working, exploring possible ways forward, choosing one, and acting on it. It involves more than finding a quick fix. It means clarifying what the real problem is, weighing your options, and checking whether your solution actually worked.

How can I improve my problem-solving skills?

Practise on real situations rather than only reading about them. Define the problem in one sentence, separate facts from assumptions, list several options before choosing, and review what happened afterwards. A problem-solving workbook gives you structured prompts to repeat this process until it becomes a habit.

How do I approach a difficult problem?

Start by slowing down. Write down what you know for certain, what you are guessing, and what limits you face. Then ask what outcome you want. Generate more than one option, test each against your constraints, and choose a small next step. Acting on something small often reveals what to do next.

How can I stop overthinking decisions?

Overthinking often means looping through the same worries without new information. Set a decision deadline, write down the two or three factors that matter most, and name what you still don't know. Then decide whether you need more information or simply a first step. The Everyday Problem-Solving Workbook includes exercises for moving from thinking to action.

Why is critical thinking important in everyday life?

Critical thinking helps you question assumptions, notice bias, and judge information before acting on it. In daily life that means better choices about work, money, study and relationships, and fewer decisions driven by habit or first impressions. It doesn't remove uncertainty, but it helps you handle it more deliberately.

What is a problem-solving workbook?

A problem-solving workbook is a guided book you write in as you read. Instead of only explaining ideas, it gives you questions, scenarios and exercises to apply to your own situations. The Everyday Problem-Solving Workbook is a 76-page paperback built around this hands-on approach.

Can problem-solving skills be learned?

Yes. Problem-solving is a set of habits: clarifying the problem, questioning assumptions, generating options, and reviewing results. Like any skill, it improves with deliberate practice and reflection. Starting with everyday situations makes it easier to build confidence before applying the approach to bigger decisions.

How can I make better decisions when I feel stuck?

Shrink the decision. Ask what is actually blocking you: missing information, fear of a wrong choice, or too many options. Then pick the smallest reversible step and try it. Looking at the problem from another perspective, such as how a friend or colleague would see it, can also help.

What are the steps of problem-solving?

A reliable sequence is: define the problem in one sentence, gather the facts and constraints, separate what you know from what you are assuming, generate several options, test each option against your constraints, choose a small next step, and review the result. The Everyday Problem-Solving Workbook walks you through this sequence across nine structured chapters.

How do I find the root cause of a problem?

Start by asking whether what you see is a symptom or the cause. Write down the facts, keep asking why it happens until you reach something you can act on, and check your assumptions and biases before you settle on an explanation. The workbook uses real scenarios to show how easily a symptom gets mistaken for the real problem.
